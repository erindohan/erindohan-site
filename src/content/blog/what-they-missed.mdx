*A guest post from Patrick.*

I’m very proud of and inspired by my wife.

She has survived much more than a brain aneurysm, and not just lived but has connected with and made a lasting impact on others. She has turned that terrible moment, and many more to follow, into a force for helping others.

She has had quite a journey and I’ve been right there by her side.

I’ve learned that “survival” is multidimensional. In a marriage or partnership, this journey might begin with one person but certainly is not one-sided.

<hr style="border: 0; border-top: 1px solid #E8DED6; margin: 2.5rem 0;" />

In a study published in the journal [Cancer](https://doi.org/10.1002/cncr.24577), researchers found that a woman was roughly six times more likely to experience partner abandonment or divorce following a serious illness than a man. Subsequent studies also confirmed this happening around the globe over the past decade.

Why does this happen?

Are men less likely to adapt to new roles in a partnership, new needs, new family requirements that weren’t there before? When the person we married changes due to a serious illness, are we looking for an unrealistic “return to normal” that never comes?

I’ve learned so much about the strength in women through Erin’s story…I know men can show up better than this (and I’m sure many have).

<hr style="border: 0; border-top: 1px solid #E8DED6; margin: 2.5rem 0;" />

There is zero doubt in my mind that I’ve had my own growth moments across Erin’s health journey, both in marriage and in fatherhood. I am a different person than I was before. I could not play the role she, or our son Trip, needed without changing significantly.

The way I think about my time changed. The responsibilities I needed to flexibly share, take on, or let go of have changed. The way I’ve had to tune in, listen, and see what my family needs of me has changed.

It is all still changing.

This growth and change is an ongoing process, parallel to Erin’s. It’s like climbing vines on a terrace. Sometimes we weave together, sometimes we part, but we are a part of the same root system and have the same goal.

Reaching towards the sun.

<hr style="border: 0; border-top: 1px solid #E8DED6; margin: 2.5rem 0;" />

The effects of a past brain aneurysm rupture can be subtle at times or in plain sight. They are always there for Erin. Part of me being aware is acknowledging that.

There have been times when I haven’t, even 7 years after her rupture, and it can feel like I’m missing a piece of the puzzle until I pause to reflect.

The way I respond to an emotional challenge, and the way my body reacts, is fundamentally different from Erin. I won’t fit the pieces together until I regain a sense of awareness and listen to the cues behind her words. This is something that takes practice and intention.

<hr style="border: 0; border-top: 1px solid #E8DED6; margin: 2.5rem 0;" />

As much as I wish a brain aneurysm rupture, miscarriage, postpartum depression and OCD, ovarian torsion, double oophorectomy, medical menopause, and lasting medical trauma didn’t happen to Erin…it all did.

All in between, her pain had debilitating and isolating effects. She lost control of her body forever—something that most just take for granted.

But there has been so much beauty on the other side.

Purpose. Impact. Strength.

<hr style="border: 0; border-top: 1px solid #E8DED6; margin: 2.5rem 0;" />

I can’t judge the relationships of others, but I can say that the husbands that left in that stat I shared probably missed out. They missed out on seeing their partner grow and change.

Erin is superhuman. She has not only become an advocate for brain aneurysm awareness and women’s health, but also founded a company, achieved early success, and continues to be a loving, present, deeply influential mother for Trip.

For me, being a part of her story has given me deeper patience, empathy, understanding, purpose, and awareness.

It can be hard to see the possibilities of those gifts when you’re watching your loved one struggle and bear down through pain, or try to survive through a medical emergency. But if you are lucky enough to make it through together, there is a powerful opportunity for growth there on the other side.
